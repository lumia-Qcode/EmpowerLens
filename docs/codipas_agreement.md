# CODIPAS vs human annotation — label agreement

Generated by `src/codipas_agreement.py` over the **2520 texts** present in both corpora. No model, no training — this compares the two label sets directly.

## Headline

| comparison | raw agreement | Cohen's kappa | 95% CI | reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| binary (distorted?) | 66.5% | **0.321** | 0.286–0.358 | fair |
| 11-class (which type?) | 36.8% | **0.199** | 0.179–0.219 | slight |
| type, among the 1030 rows BOTH call distorted | 27.5% | — | — | — |

That third row is the important one. Even where the two sources agree that a distortion is present, they disagree about **which** distortion roughly 73% of the time. The problem is therefore not a detection threshold that could be retuned — the disagreement reaches into the taxonomy itself.

## The disagreement is directional

- Human says distorted, CODIPAS says clean: **559**
- CODIPAS says distorted, human says clean: **286**

CODIPAS's derived rule under-detects distortion by roughly 2.0:1 relative to human annotators. A model trained on it inherits that conservatism, which is the most likely reason a CODIPAS-trained model scores poorly on the Annotated test set — a label-convention mismatch, not a data-volume effect.

## How to cite this

This is why the CODIPAS numbers in `results/all_experiments.csv` are **not** comparable to the Annotated ones: they are scored against a different label convention on a different test set. Report CODIPAS as a label-transfer / annotation-agreement result. A low transfer F1 is the *expected* outcome here and is not evidence that additional training data fails to help.
